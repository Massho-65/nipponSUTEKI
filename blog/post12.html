Matsushima — Why It Became One of Japan’s Three Great Views

by Kiri

Matsushima is known as one of the “Three Great Views of Japan.”

But what does that actually mean?

The title Nihon Sankei was established in the 17th century during the Edo period. A Confucian scholar named Hayashi Gahō selected three landscapes he considered the most beautiful in the country.

The three are:

  • Matsushima (Miyagi)
  • Amanohashidate (Kyoto)
  • Miyajima (Hiroshima)

These were not chosen randomly.

They represent different types of natural beauty:

  • Islands scattered across water (Matsushima)
  • A pine-covered sandbar crossing the sea (Amanohashidate)
  • A sacred island with a floating shrine (Miyajima)

The Poetry of 260 Islands

More than 260 small islands dot Matsushima Bay.

Over centuries, wind and waves shaped them into unique forms. Many are crowned with pine trees — a symbol of longevity in Japanese culture.

The famous haiku poet Matsuo Bashō visited Matsushima in 1689 during his journey recorded in Oku no Hosomichi (The Narrow Road to the Deep North).

Although legend says he was “speechless” at its beauty, he did compose poems about the region.

The idea of overwhelming beauty became part of Matsushima’s myth.

Matsushima is not just scenery — it is layered with literary and philosophical history.

The Influence of the Samurai Era

Matsushima flourished under the rule of the powerful samurai lord Date Masamune in the early 17th century.

He developed the nearby city of Sendai and helped establish Matsushima as a cultural and spiritual center.

The Zen temple Zuiganji, originally founded in 828, was restored under his patronage.

It became one of the most important Zen temples in northern Japan.

This connection between nature, Zen philosophy, and samurai authority shaped Matsushima’s identity.

Fukuura Bridge — A Modern Addition to a Historic Landscape

Among Matsushima’s many scenic elements is Fukuura Bridge.

Unlike the ancient temples and natural islands, this red bridge is relatively modern (built in 1967).

It connects the mainland to Fukuura Island and stretches about 252 meters.

While it is not ancient, it plays an important symbolic role today.

Visitors cross it to experience panoramic views of the bay.

In a landscape shaped by centuries of poetry, Zen, and samurai history, Fukuura Bridge represents how tradition and modern tourism coexist.

Kiri

What fascinates me is how Matsushima is not simply beautiful — it is curated by history. Poetry, Zen, and samurai culture all shaped this landscape.

Why Matsushima Still Captivates the World

For international visitors, Matsushima offers something uniquely Japanese:

  • Refined natural aesthetics
  • Historical continuity from the samurai era
  • Literary myth and poetic imagination
  • Harmony between human construction and nature

It is not simply a “beautiful place.”

It is a landscape curated by history.

And within that grand narrative, Fukuura Bridge stands as one chapter — a modern path across a timeless sea.
