The Secret Hidden in Tokyo Tower's Red Paint

Tokyo Tower — Part One: A Symbol of Hope Born from the Rubble

by Kiri

Many people have seen Tokyo Tower. But almost no one knows what its red color actually means.

Tokyo Tower was completed in 1958, standing 333 meters tall. At the time, it was the tallest self-supporting steel structure in the world. Now so famous as a tourist landmark, the deeper question of why it was built — in that era, in that form — has quietly faded from memory.

A Tower Built on Ruins

Tokyo in the 1950s still bore the deep scars of war. The city was slowly rebuilding from the ashes, but beneath the surface there was a fierce collective will: to create something Japan could once again be proud of — something the world would notice.

It was businessman Maeda Hisakichi who championed the idea of building the world's tallest tower in Tokyo. His insistence on surpassing the Eiffel Tower in Paris (300 m) wasn't mere vanity. It was a statement — made in steel and height — that a nation shattered by war could rise again.

The Day Tank Iron Became a Symbol of Peace

There is a fact about Tokyo Tower that is rarely told.

A portion of the steel used in its construction came from the scrapped remains of U.S. Army M4 Sherman tanks used during the Korean War (1950–1953). Iron that had rolled across battlefields was melted down, recast, and sent skyward over Tokyo as a monument to peace.

"Material created to destroy, reborn to embody hope — this story of rebirth cannot be separated from the Japan of the 1950s."

It had been just thirteen years since the end of the war. People still carried it in their bodies. That is why this tower carried meaning.

The Red and White Are Not Just a Color Scheme

Tokyo Tower's iconic red-and-white color scheme is, in fact, a legal requirement. Under Japan's Aviation Act, any structure over a certain height must be painted in high-visibility colors to prevent aircraft collisions.

And yet — the result was perfect.

To the people of that era, the sight of a lit-up Tokyo Tower floating against the Tokyo night sky was visceral proof that the future had arrived. With the 1964 Tokyo Olympics approaching and economic growth accelerating, the tower was not merely a structure — it functioned as a lighthouse, reflecting the mood of an entire age.

Who Actually Built This Tower?

One final thought — a thread to pull in Part Two.

A 333-meter steel tower, completed in just eighteen months. Even by modern construction standards, that is an extraordinary pace. The work was carried out by tobishoku — a specialized class of ironworkers, also called "sky workers" — recruited from across Japan.

No safety harnesses. Limited high-altitude scaffolding. In those conditions, they assembled steel all the way to 333 meters.

That story comes in Part Two.
