What the Poster Doesn't Show

Shinjuku Gyoen National Garden, Tokyo — Part I: The Ground Beneath

by Kiri

Before this park had cherry blossoms and postcards, it had ruins. The story of how a bombed-out imperial garden became a place that belongs to everyone.

There is a photograph you will not find in any travel guide. It shows this garden — or rather, what was left of it — in the years after the Second World War. The greenhouses: collapsed. The ornamental buildings: reduced to foundations. The manicured lawns of one of Asia's most celebrated imperial gardens: scorched and silent.

You would not recognise it as the same place.

A Privilege Transformed

The beauty is real. But so is what came before it. This matters, because when you stand among the cherry blossoms today and feel the particular calm that this garden produces, you are standing on top of that erasure.

The garden was not always open to the public. For most of its early life, it existed purely for the imperial family and their guests — a private world of exceptional horticulture. Entry was not a right. It was a privilege extended to very few.

The war ended that arrangement in a permanent way. When the question arose of what to do with the burned-out grounds, the answer that emerged was also, in its own quiet way, a political statement: open it. Make it a national park. Let anyone in.

"What had once required an imperial invitation now required only a ticket. The ground had not changed. The meaning of it had."

The Unlikely Survival

The greenhouse is perhaps the most unlikely part of this story. Originally built at the end of the 19th century, it was once considered among the finest in Asia. The building was completely destroyed during the war.

But the plants, or at least some of them, were not. The staff had saved seeds, protected cuttings, and preserved what could be preserved. When the greenhouse was eventually rebuilt in the postwar years, it reopened with living material that connected it to the institution that had burned down. A thread, very thin, had been kept intact.

This is the kind of detail that does not appear on the information boards. You walk past the greenhouse, admire the ferns, and move on. The survival story is invisible unless you already know to look for it.

Three Landscapes, One Ground

All of this happened before the garden became what it is now known for: the three distinct landscapes — the French, the English, and the Japanese sections — occupying the same ground in permanent, unresolved coexistence.

Those three gardens did not emerge from a single coherent plan. They are what survived — and what was re-imagined — after the ruin. To understand why they feel slightly like three arguments happening at once, you have to understand what the ground went through to get there.

The poster shows the blossoms. It does not show the rebuilding. That is where the more interesting story begins.
