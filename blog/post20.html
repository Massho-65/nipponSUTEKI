The Story Behind the Poster

Hiroshima Castle – And the Layers Hidden Beneath Its Silhouette

Every April, cherry blossoms drift across the wide moat like soft, gently falling snow. Carp swim gracefully on the calm surface, and the castle tower, standing atop the weathered stone walls, rests quietly against the sky.

It feels serene. Timeless.

But when I finished this poster, I realized something— there was a story that could not be contained within the frame.

A Castle Built on a Delta, a Crossroads of Power

Hiroshima Castle was built at the end of the 16th century on a vast delta where several rivers met the sea.

This location was no accident.

It was ideal for trade, movement, and control.

A castle town quickly formed, and for over two centuries, this place became the political and cultural center of the region.

The castle changed hands many times.

Each transition carried a message— this was never just a structure.

It was power, constantly redefined.

The Day the City Changed Forever

In the summer of 1945, everything changed.

A single weapon erased centuries in an instant.

The wooden keep collapsed.

Most of the buildings disappeared.

What remained were stone, scars, and water.

The moat reflected a different sky.

From that moment on, neither the city nor the castle would ever return to what they once were.

The City Chose Reconstruction

More than a decade later, a decision was made.

The castle tower would rise again.

Not as it once was, but as something that could exist in the present.

It reopened as a museum.

Gradually, gates and towers followed.

Some debate what this reconstruction means.

But for the people here, the answer is simpler.

It represents perseverance.

And peace.

My Thoughts When I Painted This

Foreground

Carp moving beneath scattered petals— life continuing quietly.

Middle Ground

Stone walls shaped by time— every surface holding memory.

Background

A castle that does not dominate, but endures.

A presence that carries both absence and resilience.

What This Poster Is

This is not a travel advertisement.

It is an invitation.

To slow down.

To observe more carefully.

If you ever stand there, watching the carp and falling petals— look up.

Think about what built this place.

What destroyed it.

And what chose to rebuild it.

Some places carry history at their core.

This is one of them.
